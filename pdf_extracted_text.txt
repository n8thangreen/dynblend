Response: The f(KL distance) term acts as the squared bias penalty in the optimization. It mathematically
enforces the trade-off between statistical uncertainty and clinical divergence.

If we derive the optimal blending weight by minimizing the expected loss (Mean Squared Error) of the
blended survival curve, we must account for both Variance and Bias (MSE=Variance+Bias2).

Assuming your external population curve Sext represents the long-term "ground truth," the bias of relying on
                                                                            ​




your extrapolated trial curve is the difference between them: Δ(t)=Sobs(t)−Sext(t).         ​   ​




When you calculate the total MSE for your blended curve Sblend=πSobs+(1−π)Sext and minimize it with respect
                                                                                    ​   ​           ​




to π, the analytical solution is:

π(t)=Vobs(t)+Vext(t)+Δ(t)2Vext(t)
            ​           ​                   ​       ​




The Δ(t)2 term is exactly where the Kullback-Leibler divergence enters. By applying a second-order Taylor
series expansion, the KL divergence between two Bernoulli distributions (your two survival probabilities at
time t) is asymptotically proportional to their squared difference weighted by the Fisher Information:

DKL(Sobs∣∣Sext)≈2Sext(1−Sext)(Sobs−Sext)2
   ​    ​           ​           ​       ​               ​       ​   ​




Therefore, the squared difference Δ(t)2 is simply a scaled version of your point-wise KL divergence:

Δ(t)2≈2×Sext(1−Sext)×DKL(Sobs∣∣Sext)
                ​           ​       ​           ​           ​




How it controls the blend: This creates a dynamic tug-of-war in the denominator of the π(t) equation.

   1.​ If the trial extrapolation closely matches the external population, the KL distance is near 0. The
       formula defaults to pure inverse-variance weighting.
   2.​ If the trial extrapolation drifts wildly away from the external population, the KL distance explodes. This
       heavily penalises the denominator.
   3.​ To maintain a high π(t) weight when the KL distance is large, the trial data must possess
       overwhelming precision (Vobs must be virtually zero) to justify the divergence.
                                                                        ​




   4.​ As soon as the trial's extrapolated variance Vobs inevitably increases, it can no longer overpower the
                                                                                ​




       KL distance penalty, and π(t) collapses to 0.

This means f(KL distance) is the mechanism that says: "I will only let you deviate from the population
baseline if you have overwhelming statistical proof. The moment your proof (precision) drops, I am pulling

you back to the baseline."

mathematically elegant choice for your dynblend package.

Because we are evaluating the distance point-wise at time t (where the state is a Bernoulli distribution: alive
with probability S, dead with probability 1−S), the math simplifies beautifully under the Wasserstein metric.

The Wasserstein Distance for Survival For two discrete Bernoulli distributions, the 1-Wasserstein distance
(W1) is simply the absolute difference between their probabilities:
       ​




W1(Sobs(t),Sext(t))=∣Sobs(t)−Sext(t)∣
   ​       ​           ​           ​       ​




This means that the squared bias term Δ(t)2 in your denominator is literally just the squared 1-Wasserstein
distance:

Δ(t)2=W12          ​




You can rewrite the automated blending weight directly in terms of Wasserstein distance without needing
any Taylor series approximations:

π(t)=Vobs(t)+Vext(t)+W1(Sobs(t),Sext(t))2Vext(t)
               ​           ​   ​       ​       ​   ​   ​




Why Wasserstein might be superior to KL for your package:

   1.​ It is symmetric: KL divergence is asymmetric—KL(Obs∣∣Ext) is different from KL(Ext∣∣Obs). This
       forces you to make a subjective choice about which curve is the "reference" or use a symmetrised
       version (like Jeffreys divergence). Wasserstein is perfectly symmetric, treating the distance between
       the two curves identically.
   2.​ It doesn't blow up at the tails (The log(0) problem): If one of your extrapolated curves drops to
       exactly S(t)=0 or 1, the log components of KL divergence shoot to infinity. This can crash the blending
       algorithm deep into the extrapolation horizon. The Wasserstein distance is strictly bounded between
       0 and 1, remaining completely stable no matter what the survival probabilities do at the tail end of the
       model.
   3.​ It maps perfectly to Mean Squared Error: Because W12 is exactly the squared error (Sobs−Sext)2, using
                                                                 ​                                ​   ​




       it directly optimizes the true Bias-Variance tradeoff of the blended curve without relying on Fisher
       Information scaling factors.

If you implement this in dynblend, you are essentially automating the blend by minimizing the
Wasserstein-penalised expected loss. It tells the algorithm: "Weight the curves by their precision, but
penalize the trial data precisely by the amount of probability mass (W1) you would have to physically move
                                                                        ​




to align it with the population baseline."

User prompt: What else can we use. Jenson Shannon etc

Response: Yes, Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) is an excellent candidate and solves the two biggest
mathematical flaws of using standard Kullback-Leibler divergence for survival extrapolation: asymmetry
and the log(0) tail explosion.

Because you are evaluating the distance point-wise at time t over a binary state (alive with probability S(t),
dead with probability 1−S(t)), several information-theoretic and probability metrics become highly tractable
for your denominator penalty term.

Here is how Jensen-Shannon and other key alternatives operate as the f(distance) penalty:

1. Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD)

JSD smooths the KL divergence by comparing both survival curves to a shared midpoint (a mixture
distribution), rather than comparing one directly to the other.

  ●​ The Mixture: M(t)=21Sobs(t)+21Sext(t)
                                ​       ​       ​           ​




  ●​ The Calculation: JSD(t)=21DKL(Sobs∣∣M)+21DKL(Sext∣∣M)
                                            ​       ​               ​           ​   ​   ​




Why it works well:

  ●​ Symmetry: JSD(Obs,Ext)=JSD(Ext,Obs). You no longer have to justify which curve is the "reference"
     baseline.
  ●​ Strictly Bounded: Unlike KL which shoots to infinity if a curve hits 0 or 1, JSD is strictly bounded
     between 0 and ln(2) (or 1 if using base-2 logs). If your trial curve drops to exactly 0 survival late in the
     horizon but the external curve is at 0.05, KL crashes your algorithm; JSD safely registers a finite
     maximum penalty.

2. Hellinger Distance

Hellinger distance is frequently used in asymptotic statistics as a more stable, symmetric alternative to KL
divergence. Instead of using logarithms (which cause the tail instability), it relies on square roots.

  ●​ The Calculation (Bernoulli):​
     H2(t)=21[(Sobs(t)−Sext(t))2+(1−Sobs(t)−1−Sext(t))2]
               ​    ​   ​   ​       ​                   ​       ​       ​   ​




Why it works well:

  ●​ Like JSD, it is symmetric and strictly bounded (between 0 and 1).
  ●​ Because it maps closely to the Bhattacharyya coefficient (measuring the exact geometric overlap of
     the two distributions), it provides a very smooth gradient. The penalty increases steadily as the
     curves diverge, making the automated π(t) transition highly predictable.

3. Total Variation Distance (TVD)

TVD measures the maximum possible difference between the probability assignments of two distributions.

  ●​ The Calculation: TVD(t)=21∑∣pi−qi∣      ​               ​   ​




Why it works well (and a mathematical shortcut): For a discrete binary outcome like point-wise survival,
the TVD formula simplifies to:

TVD(t)=21(∣Sobs−Sext∣+∣(1−Sobs)−(1−Sext)∣)
             ​           ​       ​   ​                   ​




Which collapses entirely to:

TVD(t)=∣Sobs(t)−Sext(t)∣
                 ​           ​




Notice that for this specific point-wise Bernoulli application, Total Variation Distance is mathematically
identical to the 1-Wasserstein distance. If you implement the absolute difference ∣Sobs−Sext∣ as your
                                                                                         ​   ​




penalty term, you can correctly justify it in your documentation as either the Wasserstein distance or the
TVD.

Implementing in the Formula

If you substitute any of these into your automated blending weight, you replace the squared bias term:

π(t)=Vobs(t)+Vext(t)+λ⋅Distance(t)2Vext(t)
         ​           ​                           ​   ​




(Note: You may need a scaling hyperparameter λ when swapping metrics, as a metric bounded by ln(2) like
JSD will exert a different magnitude of penalty on the denominator than the unbounded Fisher-scaled KL
divergence, altering how aggressively the algorithm switches to the external curve).

User prompt: But why are we assuming a linear barycentric

Response: We do not simply assume a linear barycenter out of convenience; it is a deliberate choice driven
by three specific statistical and clinical justifications, especially in the context of Health Technology
Assessment (HTA).

If you use a linear pool, you are making specific claims about your distance metrics, your hazard
assumptions, and your population mixture.

1. The Distance Metric Demands It The shape of a barycenter is not chosen; it is the mathematical output
of the distance metric you select to define the "center."

   ●​ If you define the information loss using Reverse KL, the optimal barycenter is geometric (log-linear).
   ●​ If you define the information loss using Forward KL or 1-Wasserstein, the mathematical solution for
      the barycenter collapses exactly to the arithmetic mean (the linear pool): Sblend=πSobs+(1−π)Sext.
                                                                                     ​      ​        ​




Therefore, by committing to a linear blend, you are implicitly stating that your optimization algorithm
penalizes distance according to Wasserstein or Forward KL principles.

2. Avoiding Hazard-Rate Assumptions In survival analysis, the mathematical scale you choose to blend on
drastically changes the clinical assumption:

   ●​ Geometric (Log-Linear) Pooling blends on the log-survival scale. Because the log of survival is the
      negative cumulative hazard (ln(S(t))=−H(t)), a geometric barycenter implies you are linearly blending
      the hazards of the two curves.
   ●​ Linear Pooling blends directly on the probability scale.

Given that dynblend was built explicitly to "smoothly bridge observed trial survival probabilities... without
relying on hazard-rate assumptions," the linear barycenter is the only pooling method that operates purely
on the survival probabilities without mathematically entangling the hazards.

3. The Population Mixture Interpretation In HTA and decision modeling, a linear combination of
probabilities formally represents a finite mixture model.

When you use a linear barycenter, the clinical interpretation of π(t) is highly intuitive: it represents the
probability that a patient's survival mechanism at time t is generated by the trial cohort's dynamics rather
than the external population's dynamics. As π(t) drops to 0, the mixture smoothly shifts until the cohort is
assumed to behave exactly like the background general population. Geometric pooling lacks this clean

demographic interpretation.

4. Mathematical Tractability of Variance To automate π(t) by minimizing the Mean Squared Error, you
must be able to calculate the variance of the blended curve. The variance of a linear blend is exact and
straightforward: Vblend=π2Vobs+(1−π)2Vext. Calculating the variance of a geometric blend requires
                            ​         ​           ​




log-transformations and Delta method approximations, which become highly unstable in the tails of the
extrapolation where survival approaches 0 (and the log approaches −∞).




User prompt: What about using bayesian posteriors

Response: Transitioning to a fully Bayesian framework is highly advantageous for Health Technology
Assessment, as it shifts the output from a single point-estimate curve with standard errors to a full
posterior distribution of Sblend(t), which plugs directly into Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis (PSA).
                                          ​




If you possess the Bayesian posteriors for both the trial extrapolation and the external population (e.g., via
MCMC draws from Stan or JAGS), the blending mechanics can be approached in three distinct ways.

1. Posterior Predictive Pooling (Sample-by-Sample Blending) Instead of calculating a single weight
based on the mean and variance, you apply the linear barycenter equation to every single MCMC iteration
independently.

For each posterior draw k:

Sblend(k)(t)=π(t)Sobs(k)(t)+(1−π(t))Sext(k)(t)
       ​              ​                       ​




You can either keep π(t) deterministic (e.g., a pre-specified decay), or you can calculate a dynamic π(k)(t) for
each draw using the distances between the specific posterior samples. By computing this across
thousands of iterations, you generate a complete posterior predictive distribution for the blended curve.
This organically captures the exploding uncertainty of the trial extrapolation and correctly propagates it
through the blend, yielding robust credible intervals for the economic model.

2. Dynamic Shrinkage (The External Curve as a Prior) Rather than fitting two separate models and
blending them post-hoc, you fit a single Bayesian survival model to the trial data where the external
population curve serves as a strongly informative, time-varying prior.

In this paradigm, π(t) ceases to be an explicit blending weight and instead emerges naturally from the
Bayesian updating mechanism:

   ●​ During the trial period: The likelihood of the observed trial data is highly concentrated. It
      overwhelms the external prior, so the posterior survival curve tracks the trial data.
   ●​ During the extrapolation period: The trial data runs out. The likelihood becomes flat and
      uninformative. By the fundamental mechanics of Bayesian inference, the posterior organically shrinks
      back toward the prior.

The "blend" happens automatically. The speed of the transition is dictated purely by the precision of the
prior versus the diminishing information in the likelihood.

3. Time-Varying Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) In standard BMA, you calculate the posterior

probability of Model A versus Model B and use those probabilities as weights. If adapted for dynamic
blending, π(t) becomes the posterior probability that the trial extrapolation is the "true" data-generating
mechanism at time t.

Sblend(t)=P(Mobs∣Data,t)Sobs(t)+P(Mext∣Data,t)Sext(t)
    ​           ​               ​               ​       ​




To make the weights time-varying, the models are evaluated dynamically (often using fractional likelihoods
or time-dependent Bayes factors). As the predictive performance of the trial extrapolation degrades in the
unobserved future, P(Mobs∣Data,t) drops toward 0, and the BMA naturally reallocates all probability mass to
                                        ​




the external baseline model.

Using Bayesian posteriors eliminates the need for Taylor series approximations of Fisher Information
because the posterior distributions inherently contain the exact geometry of the uncertainty and the
distance between the curves.




User prompt: Can I get these posterior model probs from kl still

Response: Yes, there is a direct mathematical equivalence between KL divergence and Bayesian posterior
model probabilities, bridged by a concept called Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD).

In modern Bayesian statistics (particularly when using tools like Stan or the loo package in R), you evaluate
models based on their out-of-sample predictive accuracy. The fundamental measure of this accuracy is the
KL divergence from the true data-generating process to your proposed model.

Information criteria like WAIC or LOO-CV are explicitly designed to estimate this exact KL divergence.
Therefore, you can convert KL distance directly into a Bayesian posterior model probability—often referred
to as a Pseudo-BMA weight or Akaike weight.

The Mathematical Conversion To transform a KL distance (which is unbounded) into a posterior model
probability π(t) (which is strictly bounded between 0 and 1), you pass the negative KL divergence through a
softmax function:

P(Mobs∣Data,t)=exp(−KLobs(t))+exp(−KLext(t))exp(−KLobs(t))
        ​                           ​               ​           ​   ​




Because probabilities are relative, it is mathematically identical and computationally more stable to
calculate this using the difference in KL divergence (ΔKL) between the trial extrapolation and the external
curve:

π(t)=1+exp(KLobs(t)−KLext(t))1
                    ​       ​               ​




How this automates π(t) dynamically: This equation acts as a logic gate driven by information theory.

  ●​ If the trial model captures the survival dynamics at time t better than the external curve,
     KLobs(t)<KLext(t). The exponent is negative, and π(t) shifts toward 1.
            ​           ​




  ●​ As time progresses and the trial extrapolation's variance explodes, its predictive accuracy plummets.
     KLobs(t) becomes much larger than KLext(t). The exponent becomes a large positive number, and π(t)
            ​                                               ​




     smoothly collapses to 0.

Implementation in R (dynblend / loo) If you fit your models in a Bayesian framework, you do not even
have to calculate the theoretical KL divergence by hand. Bayesian software outputs point-wise
log-likelihoods for every observation.

By calculating the point-wise Leave-One-Out Expected Log Predictive Density (LOO-ELPD) at each time t,
you are computing the exact empirical estimate of the KL divergence. You simply plug those point-wise
ELPD values into the softmax equation above (since ELPD ≈−KL). This approach is known in the Bayesian
literature as dynamic stacking or time-varying pseudo-BMA, and it perfectly operationalises your goal of
a fully automated, data-driven survival blend.




User prompt: I don't understand how bma works for different times t

Response: Standard Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) usually gives you a single, static weight for each
model across the entire dataset. To understand how BMA can give you a different weight π at every
timepoint t, we have to shift from looking at the model's global performance to its local predictive
confidence.

Here is how time-varying BMA (often called Dynamic BMA or Stacking) works, especially in the
"unobserved" extrapolation period where your dynblend package operates.

1. The Core Mechanic: Point-Wise Predictive Density

In standard BMA, a model's weight is determined by its Marginal Likelihood—how well it predicted the
entire dataset.

In time-varying BMA, we slice the timeline into individual moments t. Instead of asking "How good is this
model overall?", we ask: "If I pull a random patient out of the true population at time t, how tightly did
this model predict their exact survival status?"

This is measured by the point-wise Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD).

  ●​ If a model is highly confident and correct at time t, its predictive distribution is tightly packed around
     the true value. Its ELPD is high.
  ●​ If a model is highly uncertain, its predictive distribution spreads its probability mass out very thinly.
     Even if the mean is correct, the density at any specific point is low. Its ELPD is low.

2. How the Weights Change During Extrapolation

The magic of using this for survival blending happens in the future, beyond the trial data. At year 10, you
have no observed patients to test the models against. So how do the weights change?

They change based on the exploding uncertainty (entropy) of the extrapolated trial model.

  ●​ The External Registry Model (Mext): Because it is built on massive population data, its predictive
                                          ​




     distribution at year 10 is very tight and confident. Its point-wise predictive density remains high and
     stable.

  ●​ The Trial Extrapolation Model (Mobs): Because it is extrapolating far beyond its data, its parameter
                                                ​




     uncertainty balloons. By year 10, its predictive distribution for survival is massively wide and flat. It
     has "smeared" its probability mass over a huge range of possible outcomes.

Because the trial model's probability mass is spread so thin, its Expected Predictive Density drops
significantly compared to the external model.

3. Calculating π(t)

At every timepoint t, the algorithm calculates the expected predictive density for both models and plugs
them into the BMA softmax equation:

π(t)=exp(ELPDobs(t))+exp(ELPDext(t))exp(ELPDobs(t))
                 ​                  ​               ​   ​




The Timeline of the Blend:

  1.​ At t=1 (Inside the trial): The trial model has hard data. Its predictive density is very tight and tailored
      perfectly to the cohort. ELPDobs is much higher than ELPDext. π(t) is close to 1.
                                            ​                      ​




  2.​ At t=5 (Edge of extrapolation): The trial model's uncertainty starts to grow. Its probability mass
      begins to flatten out. ELPDobs starts dropping.
                                        ​




  3.​ At t=10 (Deep extrapolation): The trial model's variance is huge. Its density is spread incredibly thin.
      The external registry's tight density now vastly outperforms the trial model mathematically. ELPDext is ​




      much larger than ELPDobs. The equation naturally outputs a π(t) near 0.
                                ​




Why this is mathematically beautiful for dynblend

You do not have to write code that forces the weights to change over time. The formula for the BMA weight
remains exactly the same at every timepoint.

The weight π(t) changes automatically because the variance of the trial extrapolation is a function of
time. As time moves forward, the trial's variance expands, its predictive density drops, and the
time-varying BMA mathematically demotes it, organically shifting the blend to the external curve.
